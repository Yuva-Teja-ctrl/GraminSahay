Abstract—A large number of government welfare schemes exist for rural communities across agriculture, education, healthcare, housing, pensions, and employment, yet many eligible citizens never receive these benefits. The barrier is not a shortage of schemes but a lack of awareness: citizens frequently do not know which schemes they qualify for, what documents are required, how to apply, or why an earlier application was rejected, and existing government portals assume the citizen already knows a scheme’s name before they can search. This paper reviews the body of work most relevant to building a situation-aware welfare advisory system and presents GraminSahay, a multilingual, voice-first assistant that lets a citizen describe their life situation in natural language and identifies every scheme they may be eligible for. The system combines Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) over official scheme and policy documents with an eligibility-reasoning layer that classifies each scheme as eligible, possibly eligible, not eligible, or missing information, a scheme-conflict-detection stage that warns when mutually exclusive benefits are selected, and step-by-step application guidance. The literature survey covers RAG and knowledge-intensive question answering, faithfulness and automated evaluation of RAG, retrieval-augmented reasoning, multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval, domain adaptation, and the use of large language models for interpretive support in digital-government services. From this review we derive the research gaps that motivate an explainable rural welfare navigator and describe the proposed system architecture and methodology.

Keywords—Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Large Language Models, Multilingual Voice AI, Eligibility Reasoning, Scheme Conflict Detection, Rural Welfare, Public Policy Technology, Indic Speech Processing, Explainable AI.

I.  Introduction

Rural communities in India have access to a broad range of government welfare schemes spanning agriculture, education, healthcare, housing, and pensions. Despite this coverage, a large share of eligible citizens do not avail the benefits for which they qualify. The core difficulty is informational rather than administrative: citizens often do not know which schemes exist, cannot easily interpret eligibility criteria, are unsure of the documents and procedures required, and may not understand why a previous application was rejected [1].

A key limitation of existing government portals is that they are organized around scheme names. A citizen must effectively already know the name of a scheme before the portal becomes useful, which inverts the real need—citizens know their own situation, not the catalogue of schemes mapped to it. This gap is compounded by language and accessibility barriers, because most portals are primarily text-based and language-dependent, which disadvantages users who are more comfortable speaking in a regional language than reading official text [1].

Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) offers an opportunity to address this gap. RAG couples a neural retriever with a generative model so that answers are grounded in an external, authoritative document collection rather than only in the parameters of the model [8]. This grounding is particularly valuable in the welfare domain, where correctness, traceability to official sources, and the ability to update the corpus as policies change are essential. When paired with multilingual retrieval and Indic speech-to-text/text-to-speech, such a system can accept a spoken description of a citizen’s situation and respond in that citizen’s own language.

This paper presents GraminSahay, a multilingual, voice-first welfare navigator that lets a citizen describe their life situation—for example, “I am a farmer with a small landholding” or “my daughter is starting college”—and returns the schemes they may be eligible for, an explanation of the eligibility decision, warnings about schemes that cannot be claimed together, and guidance on how to apply. The focus of this paper is the literature that underpins such a system and the research gaps it must close.

II.  Literature Review

Research directly relevant to GraminSahay falls into six strands: (A) foundational Retrieval-Augmented Generation, (B) faithfulness and automated evaluation of RAG, (C) retrieval-augmented reasoning, (D) multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval, (E) dense retrieval and domain adaptation, and (F) LLMs for interpretive support in digital government. We review each strand and relate it to the welfare-navigation setting.

A. Foundational Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Lewis et al. introduced RAG for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks, combining a pretrained sequence generator with a neural retriever over an external document index [8]. Compared with a purely parametric baseline, RAG produced more specific, diverse, and factual generations and improved open-domain question answering. The central lesson for GraminSahay is that answer quality is bounded by retrieval quality; the original evaluation used a general Wikipedia index, whereas a welfare navigator must retrieve from a curated corpus of official scheme guidelines and eligibility rules.

B. Faithfulness and Automated Evaluation of RAG

Because a welfare system must not state eligibility that the retrieved evidence does not support, faithfulness is a first-class concern. Filice et al. proposed Answering with Faithfulness (AwF), which explicitly evaluates whether a generated answer is supported by the retrieved passages and shows that stronger faithfulness evaluation can improve downstream RAG performance and enable systematic fallback strategies [4]. Complementarily, RAGAs introduced reference-free metrics that separately score retrieval relevance, faithfulness, and generation quality, allowing a RAG pipeline to be evaluated without manually created ground-truth answers for every query [7]. Both lines are directly applicable to validating eligibility explanations, though automated metrics may not fully capture the correctness of legal or policy eligibility decisions.

C. Retrieval-Augmented Reasoning

Eligibility determination is a multi-step inference task, not simple lookup: a citizen’s occupation, family situation, and location must be matched against several conditions. Li et al. survey RAG-reasoning systems that combine retrieval with multi-step reasoning, including reasoning-enhanced RAG and iterative search-and-thought frameworks, and report that coupling external retrieval with reasoning improves knowledge-intensive and complex inference tasks [3]. Self-RAG lets a model decide when to retrieve and then critique both the retrieved evidence and its own output using reflection tokens, improving factuality and citation accuracy [9]. These techniques map naturally onto the eligible / possibly-eligible / not-eligible / missing-information classification, at the cost of additional retrieval and inference complexity.

D. Multilingual and Cross-Lingual Retrieval

Because GraminSahay serves citizens in English plus two regional languages, multilingual retrieval is central. Ranaldi et al. compare multilingual RAG strategies—question-translation RAG versus direct multilingual retrieval—and find that direct multilingual retrieval can improve coverage and efficiency, while translation-based retrieval may suffer coverage limitations [5]. Qi et al. study the consistency of multilingual context utilization in RAG and show that retrieved content can differ across languages, introducing cross-lingual inconsistencies [2]. For a welfare navigator, where the authoritative corpus may be predominantly in one language while queries arrive in several, these findings motivate careful alignment between the query language, the retrieval language, and the response language.

E. Dense Retrieval and Domain Adaptation

The quality of the retriever determines whether the correct scheme document is surfaced. Contriever trains dense retrievers with unsupervised contrastive learning and extends to multilingual retrieval, achieving competitive performance without supervised training and strong cross-lingual capability [10]; however, unsupervised retrieval may underperform on highly specialized government terminology without further tuning. RAFT (Retrieval-Augmented Fine-Tuning) teaches an LLM to focus on useful retrieved documents and ignore distractors in domain-specific RAG, consistently improving in-domain performance [6], at the cost of additional domain-specific training data and compute that may be difficult for a rapidly changing policy corpus.

F. LLMs for Interpretive Support in Digital Government

At the application level, a systematic review of LLMs for interpretive support in digital-government service systems analyzes sixty studies and reports a shift from simple question answering toward service navigation and interpretive support, with the strongest evidence for gains in efficiency, responsiveness, and accessibility [1]. Crucially, it notes that realized benefits depend on reliability, legal boundaries, responsibility allocation, privacy and security, and organizational capacity—non-functional requirements that any deployed welfare navigator must respect. Context-optimized RAG for local-government welfare subsidies further demonstrates the feasibility of grounding welfare answers in local policy documents.

III.  Research Gaps Identified

Scheme-Discovery Gap: Portals require the citizen to know a scheme’s name; situation-first discovery is largely unsupported [1].

Personalization Gap: Generic scheme information does not account for an individual’s occupation, family situation, or location.

Eligibility-Explanation Gap: Citizens need clear, traceable explanations of why they are (or are not) eligible, not just a yes/no verdict [4], [9].

Conflict Gap: Multiple schemes may be mutually exclusive, yet existing tools provide no integrated detection of conflicting benefits.

Accessibility Gap: Primarily text-based, single-language interfaces exclude many rural users who need multilingual, voice-based interaction [2], [5].

Guidance Gap: Users need concrete support with required documents and application steps after a scheme is identified.

Domain-Grounding Gap: General-purpose retrievers and models underperform on specialized government terminology and changing policy text without domain adaptation [6], [10].

IV.  Knowledge Sources and Data

Unlike benchmark NLP tasks, GraminSahay is grounded in an authoritative, curated corpus rather than a labelled dataset. The planned knowledge sources are:

• Official scheme guidelines for the five pilot categories—agriculture, education, healthcare, housing, and pensions—within a single state (Telangana, India).

• Eligibility-rule documents that specify conditions, thresholds, and required documents per scheme.

• Policy / circular documents capturing scheme-conflict rules (which benefits cannot be claimed together).

• Multilingual query data in English plus two regional languages (Telugu and Hindi) for voice and text input.

V.  Problem Statement

Although many welfare schemes exist and recent advances in RAG and LLMs enable grounded question answering, rural citizens still cannot reliably discover and act on the benefits they are entitled to. Existing portals are organized around scheme names, provide generic rather than personalized information, offer limited explanation of eligibility, do not detect conflicting schemes, and present significant language and accessibility barriers. There is therefore a need for an explainable, situation-aware, multilingual, voice-first navigator that understands a citizen’s situation, retrieves relevant official information, reasons about eligibility, flags scheme conflicts, and guides the citizen through application—without requiring prior knowledge of a scheme’s name.

VI.  Motivation

The practical problem is that eligible rural citizens miss out on welfare benefits primarily because of an awareness and interpretation barrier, not a lack of schemes. Existing approaches are insufficient: manual portal search assumes prior knowledge, generic information ignores individual circumstances, and text-only single-language interfaces exclude many intended beneficiaries. Advances in RAG provide an opportunity to ground answers in official documents [8]; retrieval-augmented reasoning supports multi-step eligibility inference [3], [9]; faithfulness-aware generation and automated evaluation help keep answers supported by evidence [4], [7]; and multilingual retrieval with Indic speech processing makes the system usable by voice in a citizen’s own language [2], [5]. These observations motivate a unified framework that combines situation understanding, RAG retrieval, eligibility reasoning, scheme-conflict detection, and voice-based multilingual delivery into a single explainable welfare navigator aligned with SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities).

VII.  Research Objectives

Objective 1: To develop a multilingual, voice-first interface (Indic STT/TTS, English + two regional languages) through which a citizen can describe their situation.

Objective 2: To extract a structured user profile—occupation, family situation, location, and other relevant details—from the citizen’s free-form description.

Objective 3: To build a RAG pipeline over official scheme and policy documents using a vector database for semantic retrieval.

Objective 4: To design an eligibility-reasoning layer that classifies each scheme as eligible, possibly eligible, not eligible, or missing information, with an explanation.

Objective 5: To implement scheme-conflict detection that warns when mutually exclusive benefits are selected.

Objective 6: To generate step-by-step application guidance, including required documents and links to the official portal/office, for the five pilot welfare categories.

VIII.  Existing System and Proposed System

A. Existing System

In the current approach, citizens manually search government portals and documents, typically needing to know a scheme’s name beforehand. Information is spread across multiple sources; eligibility and document requirements require manual interpretation; and personalized guidance is unavailable. The basic flow is shown in Fig. 1. Its principal limitations are manual search, limited personalization, language barriers, and no integrated conflict detection.

B. Proposed System

GraminSahay replaces name-based search with situation-first discovery, as illustrated in Fig. 2. A citizen’s spoken or typed description is converted to a structured profile; a RAG layer retrieves relevant passages from the official-document index; an LLM-based reasoning layer determines and explains eligibility; a conflict-detection layer warns about mutually exclusive benefits; and an application-guidance layer produces next steps. The final response is delivered as multilingual voice output in the citizen’s selected language.

Fig. 2.  Proposed system — GraminSahay situation-first flow.

IX.  Proposed Methodology

1) Citizen Interaction: The citizen provides input by voice (Indic STT) or text and selects a language (English + two regional). An example query is “I am a farmer with a small landholding.”

2) Profile Understanding: The system extracts a structured profile—occupation, family situation, location, and other details—from the free-form description.

3) RAG Retrieval: The profile and query are embedded and used to retrieve the top-k relevant passages from a vector database built over scheme guidelines, eligibility rules, and policy documents.

4) Eligibility Reasoning: An LLM analyzes each candidate scheme against the retrieved evidence and classifies it as eligible, possibly eligible, not eligible, or missing information, stating what additional detail is needed where applicable.

5) Scheme-Conflict Detection: The system checks selected schemes for mutual exclusivity and warns the citizen when benefits cannot legally be claimed together.

6) Application Guidance: For a chosen scheme, the system explains why it is relevant, lists required documents, outlines the step-by-step application process, and links to the official portal/office.

7) Multilingual Voice Output: The response is rendered through Indic TTS in the citizen’s selected language.

XII.  Future Enhancements

Several extensions are planned beyond the initial prototype:

• Broader coverage: Expand beyond the five pilot categories and beyond Telangana to additional states and central schemes, with a continuously updated policy corpus.

• More languages: Extend Indic STT/TTS and multilingual retrieval beyond English, Telugu, and Hindi to further regional languages and dialects.

• Document-aware applications: Let citizens upload or photograph documents (e.g., land records, ration cards) so the system can auto-verify eligibility conditions and pre-fill application forms.

• Offline / low-bandwidth mode: On-device lightweight models and cached scheme data for areas with poor connectivity.

• Live policy integration: Direct API integration with official government portals for real-time scheme updates, application submission, and status tracking.

• Explainability and trust: Source-cited answers with confidence scores and a human-in-the-loop escalation path for borderline eligibility cases.

• Accessibility: Integration with IVR / telephone and WhatsApp channels so citizens without smartphones can access the navigator.

XIII.  Conclusion

This paper reviewed the literature most relevant to building a situation-aware rural welfare navigator and derived the research gaps that motivate GraminSahay. The survey shows that RAG grounds generation in authoritative documents, that faithfulness evaluation and retrieval-augmented reasoning support trustworthy multi-step eligibility inference, that multilingual retrieval and Indic speech processing enable voice-based access in regional languages, and that domain adaptation improves performance on specialized policy text. Building on these foundations, GraminSahay combines situation understanding, RAG retrieval, eligibility reasoning, scheme-conflict detection, and multilingual voice delivery. The expected outcome is an explainable and accessible welfare assistant that helps rural citizens identify relevant government support, understand their eligibility, recognize missing information, and proceed toward application without needing prior knowledge of a scheme’s name. Future work will expand beyond the five pilot categories and the initial language set, and will report quantitative evaluation of retrieval and eligibility performance.
