ABSTRACT

India funds a long list of welfare schemes for its rural households—in farming, education, health, housing, pensions, and jobs—but a lot of people who qualify simply never receive the benefit. Usually the gap is not money; it is awareness. A citizen may not know a scheme exists, may not know what papers to carry or how to apply, and when an application is rejected the reason is rarely explained. To make matters worse, the official portals ask you to type a scheme’s name before they will tell you anything. We take the opposite starting point with GraminSahay, a multilingual, voice-first assistant: the citizen just describes their situation in everyday words, and the system works out which schemes might fit. It does this by combining Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) over official scheme and policy documents with a reasoning layer that marks each scheme as eligible, possibly eligible, not eligible, or missing information; a check that warns when two benefits cannot be claimed together; and plain, step-by-step help with applying. Before building it, we survey the research it rests on—RAG and knowledge-intensive question answering, faithfulness and its automatic evaluation, retrieval with reasoning, multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval, domain adaptation, and LLMs used to interpret government services—and from that survey we draw the gaps that motivate an explainable rural welfare navigator and sketch the architecture we propose.

Keywords: Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Large Language Models, Multilingual Voice AI, Eligibility Reasoning, Scheme Conflict Detection, Rural Welfare, Public Policy Technology, Indic Speech Processing, Explainable AI.

1. INTRODUCTION

India runs a wide range of welfare schemes for its rural population—farming support, scholarships, health cover, housing, pensions, and more. On paper the coverage looks generous. On the ground, plenty of people who clearly qualify never see a rupee of it. The reason is usually information, not administration. Someone may not know the scheme exists at all; and even when they do, the rules are hard to parse, the document checklist is vague, and a rejection letter almost never explains what actually went wrong [1].

Part of the issue is how the official portals are built. They index everything by scheme name, so a citizen has to already know what a scheme is called before the portal is of any use to them. That is backwards. People know their own circumstances; they do not carry around a mental catalogue of scheme names mapped to those circumstances. Language makes this worse: most portals are text-heavy and assume the user can read formal official prose, which leaves out anyone who would rather speak in their regional language [1].

Large language models (LLMs) and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) give us a way in. In a RAG setup, a retriever first pulls relevant passages from an external document store and a generator then answers using that evidence, so the output is tied to real sources instead of resting only on what the model memorised during training [8]. For welfare, that property matters a great deal: answers have to be correct, they have to be traceable back to an official document, and the underlying corpus has to be easy to refresh when a policy changes. Layer multilingual retrieval and Indic speech on top, and the same system can take a spoken account of someone’s situation and reply in the language they actually use.

We present GraminSahay, a multilingual, voice-first welfare navigator built around this idea. A citizen simply states their situation—say, “I am a farmer with a small landholding” or “my daughter is starting college”—and the system returns the schemes they are likely eligible for, why each decision was reached, a warning if two schemes cannot be claimed together, and the steps needed to apply. This paper concentrates on the research that such a system rests on and the gaps it still has to close.

This is more than a convenience problem. Welfare delivery sits squarely under two Sustainable Development Goals—Goal 1 (No Poverty) and Goal 10 (Reduced Inequalities)—and a scheme only protects a household once someone eligible actually claims it. So the hard part is rarely writing the policy; it is the last mile: finding the right scheme, understanding it, and surviving the application. That last mile is exactly where rural citizens stall, and it is where a good assistant can turn policy that already exists into benefits people genuinely receive—without changing a single scheme.

GraminSahay is shaped by three commitments. The first is that the citizen should never have to name a scheme; the system takes a described situation and works out the candidate schemes itself. The second is explainability—a bare yes/no verdict is close to useless, so every eligibility decision comes with a plain-language reason tied back to an official source. The third is accessibility: voice and regional-language support are treated as the main way people interact with the system, not as optional extras, because a large part of the intended audience is far more comfortable speaking than reading official documents.

2. LITERATURE SURVEY

Work on using language models for welfare and public-service help has grown out of a few separate research threads, and it is worth walking through them before explaining where our system fits. The first is Retrieval-Augmented Generation itself. Lewis et al. introduced RAG for knowledge-intensive tasks by putting a neural retriever in front of a text generator, and showed that answers grounded in retrieved passages are more specific and more factual than answers a model simply recalls from training [8]. The lesson we take from it is simple: the answer is only ever as good as what the retriever finds, and for a welfare tool the retriever has to work over official scheme and eligibility documents rather than a general web index.

A second thread is about keeping those answers honest. A welfare assistant must never promise a benefit the evidence does not support, so faithfulness matters a great deal. Filice et al. propose a method that checks whether a generated answer is actually backed by the retrieved text and show it both improves results and makes safe fallbacks possible [4]. RAGAs takes a different route, scoring retrieval relevance, faithfulness, and answer quality without needing a hand-written answer key for every question [7]. Both are useful for checking eligibility explanations, though neither can fully judge whether a policy decision is legally correct—a study of government chatbots that are deployed in practice found most are tested only for usability, not for this kind of correctness [14].

A third thread treats retrieval as part of reasoning rather than a one-off lookup, which matches our problem well, since eligibility depends on several conditions at once. Li et al. survey systems that interleave retrieval with multi-step reasoning and report clear gains on complex tasks [3]. Self-RAG lets the model decide for itself when to fetch more evidence and then criticise its own draft [9], and FLARE goes further by predicting the next sentence and retrieving again whenever it looks uncertain [18]. Because deciding eligibility is really about applying written rules to a person's facts, LegalBench is a useful warning: across 162 tasks it shows that language models handle rule-based legal reasoning unevenly [15], which is exactly why we anchor every decision in retrieved policy text instead of trusting the model's memory.

The fourth thread is multilingual retrieval, which sits at the centre of our design because we work in English, Telugu, and Hindi. Ranaldi et al. compare translating the question first against retrieving directly in each language and find the direct route usually keeps more coverage [5]. When translation cannot be avoided, IndicTrans2 offers open, good-quality translation across all 22 scheduled Indian languages, which makes that fallback practical [16]. Qi et al. caution that what RAG retrieves can quietly change from one language to another [2], and XLM-R shows a single model can transfer across many languages, with the biggest wins on low-resource ones [17]—promising for Indic text, where labelled data is thin.

Finally, retrieval quality itself depends on the retriever. Dense Passage Retrieval from Karpukhin et al. set the modern template and clearly beats older keyword baselines [12], while Contriever reaches multilingual retrieval without supervision but can trip over specialised government wording unless it is tuned [10]. RAFT trains the model to lean on the useful documents and ignore distractors, at the cost of extra training data [6]. On the Indic side, IndicNLPSuite supplies corpora, models, and a benchmark that give a sensible starting point for Telugu and Hindi [19]. On the service side, a review of language models in digital government notes a shift from plain question answering toward actually guiding people through services, as long as reliability and privacy are handled [1], and context-optimised RAG has already grounded welfare answers in local policy documents [11]. For the voice side, Whisper gives robust speech-to-text across many languages [13] and Indic-TTS supplies speech synthesis for Indian languages [20]. What none of this work does, though, is pull all of it together—understanding a described situation, retrieving the right evidence, reasoning about eligibility and explaining it, catching clashes between schemes, and answering back in the user's own spoken language. That is the gap GraminSahay is built to close.

3. GAPS IDENTIFIED

Reading across the work above, three gaps stand out. Each one gathers up several of the smaller shortcomings the earlier studies point to, and together they describe what GraminSahay has to solve.

  1. Multilingual and Voice-First Access: Most portals and prior systems are text-heavy and work in one language, which leaves out rural users who would far rather speak in their own regional language. Indic speech and cross-lingual retrieval are rarely brought into the welfare setting, so people who cannot read formal official prose are effectively shut out [1], [2], [5], [13], [17], [19].
  2. Explainable, Situation-First Eligibility: Because portals are indexed by scheme name, a citizen who does not already know the name has nowhere to begin, and a plain yes or no tells them very little. What is missing is discovery that starts from a described situation and an explanation of why a decision was reached, tied back to a trusted source [1], [4], [9], [12], [15].
  3. Application Guidance and Conflict Awareness: Finding a scheme is only half the job. Existing tools rarely warn that two benefits cannot be claimed together, and they seldom spell out which documents to gather or what the actual application steps are [1], [11].

4. PROBLEM STATEMENT

The schemes exist, and RAG and LLMs have made grounded question answering practical—yet rural citizens still struggle to find the benefits they are entitled to, let alone act on them. The portals they are handed are indexed by scheme name, show the same generic page to everyone, explain little, say nothing about schemes that clash, and are hard to use for anyone outside the main language. The need is for a navigator that is explainable, aware of the user's situation, multilingual, and voice-first: one that reads what a citizen says, pulls up the right official information, works out eligibility, flags conflicts, and walks the person through to applying—all without ever asking them to know a scheme's name first.

5. MOTIVATION

What drives this work is a plain observation: eligible rural citizens miss out mostly because they cannot find or understand the benefits, not because the benefits are missing. The usual tools do not help much. Searching a portal assumes you already know what you want; generic pages ignore who you are; and text-only, one-language interfaces leave out many of the people the schemes were written for. Recent research gives us the pieces of a better answer—RAG to anchor replies in real documents [8], retrieval with reasoning for the multi-step logic eligibility needs [3], [9], faithfulness checks to keep answers tied to the evidence [4], [7], and multilingual retrieval with Indic speech to make it work by voice in a person's own language [2], [5]. Our goal is to join these pieces into one explainable welfare navigator, something that speaks directly to SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities).

6. RESEARCH OBJECTIVES

The work is organised around six concrete objectives.

Objective 1: Build a voice-first, multilingual front end—Indic STT/TTS in English, Telugu, and Hindi—so a citizen can simply say what their situation is.

Objective 2: Turn that free-form description into a structured profile: occupation, family situation, location, and whatever else bears on eligibility.

Objective 3: Stand up a RAG pipeline over the official scheme and policy documents, backed by a vector database for semantic retrieval.

Objective 4: Add a reasoning layer that labels each scheme eligible, possibly eligible, not eligible, or missing information—and says why.

Objective 5: Detect scheme conflicts, so the citizen is warned when two benefits cannot be claimed together.

Objective 6: Produce step-by-step application guidance for the five pilot categories, listing the documents required and pointing to the official portal or office.

7. EXISTING SYSTEM AND PROPOSED SYSTEM

A. Existing System

As things stand, a citizen has to hunt through government portals and documents by hand, usually needing the scheme’s name just to begin. The relevant details are scattered across several places, eligibility and document requirements have to be worked out by reading the fine print, and nobody tailors any of it to the individual. Fig. 1 shows this flow. Its weak points are not subtle: the search is manual, there is almost no personalisation, language is a barrier, and nothing warns you when two schemes conflict.

Fig. 1.  Existing system — manual welfare scheme lookup.

B. Proposed System

GraminSahay swaps name-based search for situation-first discovery (Fig. 2). Whatever the citizen says or types becomes a structured profile. From there a RAG layer fetches the relevant passages out of the official-document index, an LLM reasoning layer decides eligibility and explains it, a conflict-detection layer raises a flag when two benefits are mutually exclusive, and a guidance layer lays out what to do next. The answer comes back as multilingual voice in whichever language the citizen chose.

Fig. 2.  Proposed system — GraminSahay situation-first flow.

8. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY

1) Citizen Interaction: Input arrives either by voice (through Indic STT) or as text, with the citizen picking a language—English, Telugu, or Hindi. A typical opening line is “I am a farmer with a small landholding.”

2) Profile Understanding: From that free-form sentence the system pulls out a structured profile: occupation, family situation, location, and any other detail that happens to matter.

3) RAG Retrieval: The profile and the query are embedded together and used to fetch the top-k passages from a vector database built over the scheme guidelines, eligibility rules, and policy documents.

4) Eligibility Reasoning: An LLM weighs each candidate scheme against the retrieved evidence and tags it eligible, possibly eligible, not eligible, or missing information—and where something is missing, it says what else it would need to know.

5) Scheme-Conflict Detection: The chosen schemes are checked against one another, and the citizen is warned whenever two benefits cannot legally be held together.

6) Application Guidance: Once a scheme is picked, the system explains why it fits, lists the documents needed, spells out the application steps, and links through to the official portal or office.

7) Multilingual Voice Output: Finally the reply is spoken back through Indic TTS in whichever language the citizen selected.

8) Evaluation: We will judge the pipeline on three fronts—retrieval relevance, answer faithfulness, and eligibility-classification quality—using reference-free RAG metrics [7] alongside faithfulness evaluation [4]. Concretely, retrieval is scored with Recall@k and mean reciprocal rank over labelled situation–scheme pairs; classification with per-class precision, recall, and macro-F1 across the four labels; and faithfulness as the fraction of justifications that the retrieved passages fully back up. On top of the automated numbers, a small panel of domain reviewers will rate how clear and how correct the explanations are.

Two concerns run across the whole design. One is keeping the data fresh: schemes and thresholds move, so the corpus is versioned and re-indexed on a schedule, and every answer notes the document version it leaned on. The other is privacy: we keep the citizen profile deliberately thin—only the attributes eligibility actually needs—and nothing sensitive is held past the session unless the citizen opts in, which lines up with the governance conditions the digital-government literature stresses [1].

C. Discussion of Expected Outcomes

Taken together, Tables I and II and Figs. 4 and 5 describe how we want the system to behave. The healthcare and education numbers in Table I are kept deliberately modest: eligibility there hinges on several conditions at once—income bands, institution type, age—which is simply harder to get right than, say, a pension scheme with fairly uniform rules. Table II, meanwhile, is about the difference a citizen would actually feel: the manual process offers no situation-first discovery, no explainable eligibility, and no conflict detection, while the proposed system offers all three. Fig. 5 puts the main tension on the table—more passages mean higher recall but a smaller fraction of the answer that is strictly backed by evidence—and that is what pushes us toward k ≈ 5. We treat these figures as acceptance thresholds rather than aspirations: a configuration passes only if it hits the tabulated accuracy and faithfulness and keeps end-to-end latency low enough to feel interactive over voice.

11. FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS

Once the prototype is working, there are several directions we would like to take it.

• Broader coverage: Push past the five pilot categories and past Telangana—to more states and to central schemes—keeping the policy corpus continuously up to date.

• More languages: Grow the Indic STT/TTS and multilingual retrieval beyond English, Telugu, and Hindi into further regional languages and dialects.

• Document-aware applications: Allow citizens to upload or photograph documents such as land records or ration cards, so the system can check eligibility conditions automatically and even pre-fill parts of the form.

• Offline / low-bandwidth mode: Run lightweight models on-device and cache scheme data, so the navigator still works where connectivity is poor.

• Live policy integration: Wire the system directly into government portal APIs for real-time scheme updates, application submission, and status tracking.

• Explainability and trust: Attach source citations and confidence scores to every answer, and route borderline eligibility cases to a human for review.

• Accessibility: Reach citizens without smartphones by adding IVR / telephone and WhatsApp channels.

12. CONCLUSION

This paper reviewed the research closest to a situation-aware rural welfare navigator and used it to pin down the gaps GraminSahay sets out to close. A few points come through clearly. RAG keeps answers tied to authoritative documents; faithfulness checks and retrieval-with-reasoning together make multi-step eligibility decisions trustworthy; multilingual retrieval and Indic speech open the door to voice access in regional languages; and some domain adaptation is what keeps all of this working on dense policy text. GraminSahay brings these together into one flow—understanding a situation, retrieving evidence, reasoning about eligibility and explaining it, catching conflicts, and replying in multilingual voice. The result we are aiming for is an explainable, accessible assistant that helps a rural citizen find the right support, understand whether they qualify, see what is still missing, and move toward applying—without needing to know a scheme's name to start. The next steps are to extend beyond the five pilot categories and the first set of languages, and to replace the expected numbers with measured ones.
